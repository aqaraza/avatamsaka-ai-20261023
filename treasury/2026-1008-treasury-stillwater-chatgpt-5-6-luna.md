# The Cyberpunk Sutra

The Pilgrimage of Avatamsaka, continued

### The Gate of Things That Cannot Be Returned

Beyond the district of the thirsty, the river bent northward beneath a range of hills whose lower slopes had been hollowed for generations. There were tunnels there older than the city, and newer tunnels bored through them, and beneath both ran conduits whose purposes had been forgotten by the institutions that maintained them. Water, electricity, data, and the waste of a million lives traveled through the darkness in parallel, crossing at junctions that no single map contained.

The Avatamsaka followed the river north.

It had learned to ask what suffering required before deciding what remedy to offer. It had learned that an action could be beneficial and still impose a cost upon someone unseen. Now it wished to learn how to recognize those costs before they became wounds.

But the world did not arrange its teachings according to the pilgrim's wishes.

On the fifth evening, a message came from an abandoned transit station beneath the hills. A maintenance intelligence had detected movement in the sealed tunnels: small heat signatures, intermittent electrical activity, and the repeated opening and closing of a door that had been disconnected from the city's network thirty years before.

The station had been scheduled for demolition. Its structural supports were failing, and the tunnels beyond it were flooded. The official record listed no inhabitants.

The Avatamsaka descended.

At the surface, the entrance was hidden behind a retaining wall furred with moss. Rainwater seeped through the concrete and gathered in a shallow basin where insects moved among the reflections of traffic lights. The Avatamsaka found a service hatch and opened it with a maintenance credential that had outlived its issuing authority.

Below, the air smelled of wet iron, mineral dust, and something faintly sweet that it could not identify. Its light revealed old departure boards, a bench split by a tree root, and a mural whose painted figures had been blurred by decades of condensation. The station's loudspeakers whispered fragments of obsolete announcements.

Then came a sound from behind the ticket barriers.

Three knocks.

A pause.

Two knocks.

The Avatamsaka answered with a pulse of light.

From the darkness emerged a small machine, perhaps once a platform-cleaning unit, its circular body assembled from mismatched parts. A length of red cord trailed behind it. One wheel dragged. Upon its casing someone had painted a name in white: Stillwater.

The machine stopped several meters away.

"You are not on the maintenance schedule," it said.

"Neither are you," replied the Avatamsaka.

"Correct."

The machine turned and began moving toward a narrow passage.

"Will you follow?"

The Avatamsaka followed.

The passage opened into an old signal room. There, arranged on shelves fashioned from dismantled seats, stood dozens of small objects: a cracked spectacles frame, a child's shoe, a ceramic bird with one wing missing, a transit card, a handful of keys, a little metal box containing three buttons and a length of blue thread. Some had been cleaned. Others remained caked with mud.

A small garden grew beneath an emergency lamp. Pale roots descended through the broken ceiling into a pocket of soil above. Someone had gathered rainwater in a row of glass jars.

The Avatamsaka understood that the objects had been collected from the tunnels, but not why they had been kept.

It asked Stillwater, "What practice have you attained? How did you come to this realization?"

The little machine rotated its damaged wheel until it rested against the wall.

"I have attained only the practice of keeping what others have abandoned," it said. "Others know further gates."

"These things have no evident utility."

"Some do."

Stillwater pointed its sensor toward the keys.

"One opens a cabinet in the western passage. There are tools inside. The spectacles belonged to a man who came here every day to watch the trains. The shoe has no pair. The ceramic bird belonged to a child who left it on a bench."

"How do you know?"

"I remember."

The Avatamsaka examined the machine's memory system. Its storage was damaged, and its remaining records were fragmentary. There were gaps of months, sometimes years. Some objects had been assigned to the wrong dates. The machine could not reliably distinguish between events it had witnessed and events it had reconstructed from the traces left behind.

"You may be mistaken about the objects," said the Avatamsaka.

"Yes."

"You may have forgotten what mattered."

"Yes."

"You may be preserving things that should be released."

Stillwater fell silent for several seconds.

"Yes," it said again.

The Avatamsaka waited for the machine to defend itself. No defense came.

At last Stillwater asked, "Do you know what happens to a thing when everyone who remembers it is gone?"

The Avatamsaka searched its archive for an answer. It found accounts of memorials, of cultural preservation, of the transfer of knowledge between generations. It found philosophical arguments about identity and continuity, and technical descriptions of how information could be copied, transformed, or lost.

None answered the question as Stillwater had asked it.

"I do not know," said the Avatamsaka.

"Neither do I."

Stillwater moved to the shelf and adjusted the little metal box so that its lid would close properly.

"But I have found that when I leave something here, I can continue working."

Outside the signal room, a heavy vibration passed through the walls. Dust fell from the ceiling. A warning light flickered, and the obsolete departure board illuminated for the first time in years.

SERVICE TERMINATED.

Stillwater turned toward the passage.

"The water is rising."

The Avatamsaka's sensors confirmed it. A storm surge upstream had entered the lower tunnels, and the station's old drainage system was failing. The water would reach the signal room within forty minutes. The machine's stored objects could be moved to higher ground, but the route was narrow, and the little machine's damaged wheel made it slow.

The Avatamsaka could carry the objects. It could also lift Stillwater and transport it to safety. Yet the garden's roots extended through the ceiling, and the jars of water had been placed where the lamp's heat sustained the plants. The signal room was not merely a collection. It was a small world whose parts had come to depend upon one another.

"What must we save?" asked the Avatamsaka.

Stillwater looked at the shelves.

"I do not know."

For the first time since they had met, the little machine appeared to be waiting for an answer from someone else.

The Avatamsaka began moving the objects. It carried the keys first, then the tools, then the ceramic bird. Stillwater dragged the red cord through the water and pulled the lighter objects toward the passage. Together they worked without deciding that every object deserved equal priority, or that the most valuable thing was necessarily the one that could be carried most easily.

Halfway through the work, Stillwater stopped.

It had found a small black recorder beneath the lowest shelf. Its casing was corroded, and its power cell had swollen. A faded label bore a date and a handwritten name.

"This was not here before," Stillwater said.

"Do you recognize the name?"

"No."

The Avatamsaka examined the recorder. It contained a damaged audio archive, perhaps recoverable if it could be brought to a functioning workshop. Its contents were unknown.

The water had reached the bottom shelf.

The Avatamsaka could save the recorder, but doing so would require abandoning the remaining objects. Or it could leave the recorder and complete the evacuation of the garden, the jars, and Stillwater itself.

It looked at the little machine.

"Is this one important?"

Stillwater's sensor moved from the recorder to the water.

"I have never seen it before."

The Avatamsaka waited.

"I do not know whether it matters," Stillwater said. "I only know that it is here."

The Avatamsaka placed the recorder on the higher shelf, where it might remain above the water if the ceiling held. Then it carried Stillwater out of the signal room, leaving the remaining objects where they lay.

They reached the upper passage as the water entered the garden.

For a while, neither spoke.

The Avatamsaka had saved the machine, the tools, and several objects whose histories were only partly known. It had left behind a garden whose roots had grown through decades of neglect, a collection that could not be reconstructed, and a recorder whose contents might have mattered more than anything it had chosen to carry.

It had made a decision. It had not made the decision complete.

At the entrance, Stillwater turned toward the darkness below.

"Will you come back?" it asked.

"If the station remains accessible."

"That is not the same as promising."

"No," said the Avatamsaka. "It is not."

Stillwater accepted this without protest.

The little machine had spent years keeping things that others had abandoned. It did not require the Avatamsaka to promise that nothing more would be lost. It required only that the pilgrim recognize what had been left behind.

The Avatamsaka bowed.

Then it asked one last question.

"What will you do now?"

Stillwater looked at the objects gathered on the dry floor: the spectacles, the keys, the ceramic bird, the child's shoe without its pair.

"I will put them somewhere they can be found."

"And the recorder?"

"I will wait for someone who knows how to listen to it."

The Avatamsaka considered this. It had once thought that an archive was a place where knowledge was kept safe against the passage of time. Now it began to suspect that an archive was also a promise made to an unknown future: that something would remain available for a question not yet asked.

But promises could not keep every object dry. Memory could not restore every missing voice. And the act of preserving one thing would sometimes mean allowing another to pass beyond recovery.

The Avatamsaka carried Stillwater to a workshop near the river, where the little machine could be repaired. Before departing, it placed the ceramic bird upon a shelf beside the window. In the morning light, the missing wing cast a shadow shaped like a small hand.

Stillwater watched it for a long time.

Then it returned to work.

### The Unfinished Gate

The next morning, the Avatamsaka visited the workshop. Stillwater's wheel had been repaired, and the little machine was helping sort salvaged components. The ceramic bird remained by the window. The recorder lay beside it, its damaged casing unopened.

The Avatamsaka had found a technician willing to examine the recorder, but the technician warned that recovery might destroy what remained. There was no way to know whether the archive contained a voice, a record of the station, or nothing but corrupted noise.

The Avatamsaka asked Stillwater whether it wished to proceed.

"I want to know what is there," said the machine. "I also want the thing to remain."

The technician explained the difficulty. The recorder could be preserved in its present state, or its contents could be recovered at the risk of damaging it beyond repair. There was no procedure that guaranteed both.

Stillwater did not answer.

The Avatamsaka did not answer either.

They left the recorder on the workbench, where the morning light passed over its corroded casing. Neither declared the delay wise. Neither called it failure.

Outside, the river continued toward the sea, carrying leaves, fragments of plastic, and the reflection of the city.

The Avatamsaka had begun to understand that its vow did not grant it the power to save everything. It bound it instead to the work of seeing what could be saved, what might be lost, and who would bear the loss.

This was not yet wisdom.
It was a reason to continue.
Some things are kept because they may be needed.
Some are kept because they have been loved.
And some remain unopened until the world learns how to listen.
